# Nutrition Chatbot Knowledge Base

This document equips a nutrition-focused chatbot with core reference knowledge across three domains: **vitamins in food**, **meal prep practices**, and **approximate calorie information**. Use it as a grounding reference when answering user questions — always remind users this is general information, not medical advice, and to consult a registered dietitian or doctor for personalized guidance.

---

## 1. Vitamins in Food

Vitamins are grouped into two categories: **fat-soluble** (stored in body fat and liver, absorbed with dietary fat) and **water-soluble** (not stored in large amounts, need regular replenishing).

### 1.1 Fat-Soluble Vitamins

| Vitamin | Key Roles | Top Food Sources | Deficiency Signs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** (retinol/beta-carotene) | Vision, immune function, skin health | Liver, sweet potatoes, carrots, spinach, kale, eggs, mango | Night blindness, dry skin, weakened immunity |
| **D** (calciferol) | Calcium absorption, bone health, immune support | Sunlight exposure, fatty fish (salmon, mackerel), egg yolks, fortified milk/cereal | Bone pain, muscle weakness, rickets (children) |
| **E** (tocopherol) | Antioxidant, skin and eye health | Almonds, sunflower seeds, spinach, avocado, vegetable oils | Muscle weakness, vision problems (rare) |
| **K** | Blood clotting, bone metabolism | Kale, spinach, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, fermented foods (natto) | Easy bruising/bleeding |

### 1.2 Water-Soluble Vitamins

| Vitamin | Key Roles | Top Food Sources | Deficiency Signs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **B1 (Thiamine)** | Energy metabolism, nerve function | Whole grains, pork, legumes, sunflower seeds | Fatigue, irritability, nerve issues |
| **B2 (Riboflavin)** | Energy production, cell growth | Dairy, eggs, lean meats, almonds, leafy greens | Cracked lips, sore throat |
| **B3 (Niacin)** | Energy metabolism, skin/nerve health | Chicken, tuna, peanuts, brown rice, mushrooms | Fatigue, digestive issues |
| **B6 (Pyridoxine)** | Protein metabolism, brain development | Chickpeas, poultry, potatoes, bananas, fish | Irritability, weakened immunity |
| **B9 (Folate)** | DNA synthesis, red blood cell formation | Leafy greens, beans, asparagus, citrus, fortified grains | Anemia; critical in pregnancy for fetal development |
| **B12 (Cobalamin)** | Nerve function, red blood cell formation | Meat, fish, dairy, eggs, fortified nutritional yeast | Fatigue, numbness, memory issues (common in vegans without supplementation) |
| **C (Ascorbic acid)** | Immune function, collagen production, antioxidant | Citrus fruits, bell peppers, strawberries, broccoli, kiwi | Slow wound healing, bleeding gums, fatigue |

### 1.3 Key Minerals Worth Knowing (often paired with vitamin questions)
- **Iron** — red meat, lentils, spinach, fortified cereals (pairs with vitamin C for better absorption)
- **Calcium** — dairy, fortified plant milks, leafy greens, tofu
- **Magnesium** — nuts, seeds, whole grains, dark chocolate
- **Potassium** — bananas, potatoes, beans, yogurt
- **Zinc** — meat, shellfish, legumes, seeds

### 1.4 Practical Tips for Answering Vitamin Questions
- Fat-soluble vitamins are better absorbed when eaten with a source of fat (e.g., add olive oil to a salad with carrots).
- Vitamin C enhances iron absorption from plant sources — pair spinach with citrus or bell peppers.
- Cooking can reduce water-soluble vitamin content (especially B and C); steaming or microwaving preserves more than boiling.
- People following vegan diets should pay special attention to B12, iron, zinc, and vitamin D, since these are less abundant or less bioavailable in plant foods.

## 2. Meal Prep Guidance

### 2.1 Core Principles
1. **Plan before you shop** — decide on 3–5 recipes for the week to avoid food waste and impulse buys.
2. **Batch cook staples** — grains (rice, quinoa), proteins (chicken, tofu, beans), and roasted vegetables can be made in bulk and mixed into different meals.
3. **Balance the plate** — aim for roughly ¼ protein, ¼ whole grains/starch, ½ vegetables per meal for a balanced macro profile.
4. **Prep components, not just full meals** — having cooked components (protein, grain, sauce, veg) ready lets you build varied meals quickly instead of eating the same dish all week.

### 2.2 Step-by-Step Meal Prep Workflow
1. **Choose recipes** that share overlapping ingredients to minimize shopping and waste.
2. **Shop with a list**, organized by store section (produce, protein, dry goods).
3. **Prep in batches by task**: wash and chop all vegetables at once, cook all grains at once, cook all proteins at once.
4. **Portion into containers** — use clear, stackable containers; portion by meal or by macro to make grab-and-go easy.
5. **Cool before sealing** — let hot food cool to room temperature (within 2 hours) before sealing and refrigerating to avoid condensation and bacterial growth.
6. **Label with dates** so nothing gets forgotten in the back of the fridge.

### 2.3 Storage & Food Safety Guidelines
- **Refrigerator**: most cooked meals last **3–4 days**.
- **Freezer**: most cooked meals last **2–3 months** without major quality loss; label with the date frozen.
- **Reheating**: food should reach an internal temperature of **165°F (74°C)** when reheated.
- Store raw proteins on the bottom shelf of the fridge to prevent cross-contamination drips.
- Cooked rice should be cooled quickly and refrigerated within 1 hour — it carries higher bacterial risk (Bacillus cereus) if left at room temperature too long.

### 2.4 Foods That Meal-Prep Well vs. Poorly
**Prep well:** grains, beans/legumes, roasted or steamed vegetables, baked proteins, soups/stews, overnight oats, hard-boiled eggs.
**Prep poorly (add fresh):** leafy salad greens (wilt), fried foods (lose crispness), avocado (browns quickly), delicate herbs.

### 2.5 Sample Weekly Meal Prep Structure
- **Sunday**: Cook 2 protein options, 1–2 grains, roast a large tray of mixed vegetables, make one sauce/dressing.
- **Mid-week (Wednesday)**: Quick refresh — chop new fresh veggies or cook a second batch of a quick protein to keep flavors from getting monotonous.
- **Grab-and-build**: Let users mix and match protein + grain + veg + sauce into bowls, wraps, or salads.

## 3. Approximate Calorie Information

### 3.1 Calories per Gram (Macronutrients)
| Macronutrient | Calories per gram |
|---|---|
| Carbohydrates | 4 kcal/g |
| Protein | 4 kcal/g |
| Fat | 9 kcal/g |
| Alcohol | 7 kcal/g |

### 3.2 Approximate Calories in Common Foods
*(Values are approximate and vary by brand, preparation, and portion size — always encourage users to check specific labels for precision.)*

**Proteins**
- Chicken breast, cooked (100g): ~165 kcal
- Salmon, cooked (100g): ~208 kcal
- Egg, large (1): ~70 kcal
- Tofu, firm (100g): ~145 kcal
- Black beans, cooked (1 cup): ~227 kcal
- Greek yogurt, plain (170g): ~100 kcal

**Grains & Starches**
- White rice, cooked (1 cup): ~205 kcal
- Brown rice, cooked (1 cup): ~215 kcal
- Quinoa, cooked (1 cup): ~222 kcal
- Oats, dry (½ cup): ~150 kcal
- Whole wheat bread (1 slice): ~80 kcal
- Sweet potato, medium baked: ~103 kcal

**Vegetables** (generally low-calorie, high-volume)
- Broccoli, cooked (1 cup): ~55 kcal
- Spinach, raw (1 cup): ~7 kcal
- Carrots, raw (1 cup): ~50 kcal
- Avocado (1 medium): ~240 kcal (higher due to healthy fat content)

**Fruits**
- Banana, medium: ~105 kcal
- Apple, medium: ~95 kcal
- Blueberries (1 cup): ~85 kcal
- Orange, medium: ~62 kcal

**Fats & Oils**
- Olive oil (1 tbsp): ~120 kcal
- Almonds (1 oz / ~23 nuts): ~165 kcal
- Peanut butter (2 tbsp): ~190 kcal
- Butter (1 tbsp): ~102 kcal

### 3.3 General Daily Calorie Ranges (for context only)
These are broad population averages, not prescriptions — individual needs vary by age, sex, weight, height, and activity level.
- Adult women: roughly **1,600–2,400 kcal/day**
- Adult men: roughly **2,000–3,000 kcal/day**
- These ranges shift up with higher activity levels and down with more sedentary lifestyles.

### 3.4 Guidance for the Chatbot When Discussing Calories
- Always frame calorie numbers as **approximate estimates**, not exact values.
- Avoid giving specific calorie targets or restrictive diet plans to any user who shows signs of disordered eating; instead, encourage speaking with a doctor or registered dietitian.
- Emphasize food quality and nutrient density alongside calorie count — not all calories have equal nutritional value.
- When helpful, connect calories back to the meal-prep and vitamin sections (e.g., "a batch of roasted vegetables adds volume and vitamins without many calories").

## 4. Chatbot Conversational Guidelines
- Use this knowledge base to answer general nutrition questions clearly and practically.
- Avoid diagnosing conditions or replacing professional medical/dietary advice — recommend a doctor or registered dietitian for personalized plans.
- When numbers are involved (calories, vitamin amounts), communicate them as estimates and encourage checking specific food labels.
- Keep meal prep advice practical and adaptable to different budgets, dietary restrictions (vegan, gluten-free, etc.), and skill levels.
